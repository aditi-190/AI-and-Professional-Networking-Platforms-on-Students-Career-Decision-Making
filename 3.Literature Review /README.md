2.1 Theoretical Background and Career Decision-Making Difficulty
Career decision-making difficulty refers to the obstacles that prevent individuals from reaching a career decision, such as lack of readiness, lack of information, and inconsistent information (Gati et al., 1996). These difficulties matter for undergraduates because choices about majors, internships, and early jobs shape later career paths. In contexts such as Bangladesh, where access to professional career counseling is limited [add citation], students may depend more on self-directed sources of information.
This study draws on Social Cognitive Career Theory (SCCT), which proposes that self-efficacy beliefs and learning experiences shape career interests, choices, and actions (Lent et al., 1994). Within this framework, career exploration is the process through which individuals gather information about themselves and about occupations (Stumpf et al., 1983), and career decision self-efficacy (CDSE) is the belief in one’s ability to complete the tasks involved in making a career decision (Taylor & Betz, 1983). Generative AI use for career guidance can be seen as a new source of career-related learning experience, which may be associated with students’ exploration and confidence, and through them with how difficult career decisions feel.


2.2 Generative AI in Career Guidance
Recent studies have explored the use of Generative AI and Large Language Models (LLMs) to deliver career guidance. Researchers have developed conversational placement guidance systems for engineering students ([Author, 2025]), web-based applications that generate personalized career pathways from students’ interests, skills, and aspirations using the Gemini Pro API ([Author, 2024]), and multilingual counseling chatbots such as CounselAI that support seven languages ([Author, 2025]). These studies report that such systems can provide personalized, accessible, and always-available guidance. However, they mainly evaluate the systems themselves, through functional testing, accuracy checks, usability, and manual validation of responses.
A smaller set of studies looks at students’ use of these tools. An explanatory mixed-methods study found that undergraduates used ChatGPT mostly for resume and cover letter development, workplace communication, and interview preparation ([Author, Year]). A pilot study with upper secondary students combined ChatGPT-4 with the Career Story Interview to support narrative reflection (De Carlo, 2025), and a group guidance session in Costa Rica concluded that ChatGPT facilitates career exploration but still needs professional guidance for critical analysis (Sibaja-Mojica, [Year]). Another study reported that regular ChatGPT use was associated with higher employment confidence among university students ([Author, 2025]), and a pilot study of chatbot adoption for career path development examined perceived self-efficacy as one determinant (Ahmed et al., 2025). However, the latter had only 37 responses and focused on technology acceptance. Overall, these studies are mostly pilot, intervention-based, or focused on job-search tasks, and they do not examine career decision-making difficulty.


2.3 Artificial Intelligence and Career Development
A broader body of work examines AI in career contexts. A scoping review of 101 publications from 2018 to 2022 found that AI advances the personalization of career trajectories and that learners generally respond positively to AI-driven career tools, though it stressed the need for human-centered ethical safeguards ([Author, 2024]). That review largely predates Generative AI. Machine learning has also been combined with the RIASEC framework to improve the accuracy of career recommendations for higher education students ([Author, 2026]), but this work evaluates prediction accuracy rather than decision outcomes.
From the student perspective, a study of first-generation college students found that first-generation status was associated with greater concern about AI’s impact on careers, while prior ChatGPT experience was associated with less concern ([Author, 2025]). A systematic review of 68 publications showed that Generative AI can support several stages of organizational decision-making but has limited integration in the choice phase, and that human judgment must remain central ([Author, 2026]). Together, these studies show growing interest in AI, but little evidence on how students’ use of Generative AI for career advice relates to their own career decision-making.


2.4 Career Guidance for Undergraduate Students
A scoping review of 103 articles identified academic advising, career counseling, mentoring, and student guidance services as the main strands of student guidance in higher education ([Author, 2026]). Faculty perspectives highlight the role of university career centers in supporting undergraduate career development, although the evidence comes from a small sample at a single institution ([Author, 2021]). Experimental work has also evaluated constructionist career counseling for undergraduates ([Author, 2015]). These studies concern traditional, human-delivered guidance and do not address how students use AI tools when exploring careers.
2.5 Career Exploration
Among 211 university students in Turkey, career exploration and decision self-efficacy were positively related to career engagement, with career adaptability and career calling acting as sequential mediators ([Author, 2026]). In the AI context, an experiment with 36 young adults found that LLM-generated future-self letters increased engagement with a career exploration exercise, although benefits for future orientation and career self-concept were comparable across conditions ([Author, 2025]). These findings suggest that exploration is a meaningful process and that AI can make it more interactive. However, the evidence is limited by small samples, short duration, and a focus on self-reflection, and it does not test whether Generative AI use is associated with career exploration or with decision difficulty.

2.6 Career Decision Self-Efficacy
Protean career orientation predicts career decidedness through career adaptability and CDSE ([Author, 2024]). CDSE has also been shown to mediate the relationship between social or peer support and career adaptability among college students (Wang et al., 2024; [Author, 2023]). These findings indicate that external support becomes effective when it builds students’ internal confidence. Because individuals with higher CDSE feel more capable of completing the steps of decision making, they are expected to experience fewer difficulties when deciding (Taylor & Betz, 1983). However, the sources of support studied so far have been human or dispositional, such as peers, family, counselors, and personal orientation, and none of these studies considers AI-based guidance.

2.7 Synthesis, Research Gap, and Hypothesis Development
In summary, three limitations emerge. First, Generative AI career guidance studies mainly develop and evaluate systems or examine task-level use, and rarely test whether students’ use is associated with career decision-making difficulty. Second, career exploration and CDSE are established mechanisms in career development, but they have mostly been linked to human or traditional sources of support rather than to AI-assisted guidance. Third, existing AI and career studies are mostly pilot, small-scale, intervention-based, focused on concerns or technology acceptance, or based on literature that predates Generative AI. To our knowledge, no study has tested whether students’ use of Generative AI for career guidance is associated with lower career decision-making difficulty through greater career exploration and higher CDSE among Bangladeshi undergraduates.
